Project 1 — DS2

F1 2021 Season Analysis

Exploratory data analysis using results.csv and races.csv

01 — Problem Definition

The 2021 Formula 1 season was one of the most exciting in years. Max Verstappen and Lewis Hamilton were fighting for the championship all the way to the very last race of the season. I got curious — what does the actual data say about how that happened? Is it just about having the fastest car, or do other things like starting position and reliability play a role too?

To explore this I asked three simple but meaningful questions:

  • ?Who scored the most points across the entire 2021 season?
  • ?Does where you start a race affect where you finish?
  • ?Which drivers made it to the podium the most often?

These questions are interesting because F1 is not just about raw speed. Strategy, consistency, and reliability all shape the final standings. Anyone who follows motorsport — fans, journalists, or the teams themselves — would find value in understanding these patterns.

The dataset comes from Kaggle and is built from the Ergast Motor Racing API — a publicly available database of Formula 1 results going back to 1950. I only used two files.

results.csv is the main file. Each row represents one driver's result in one race — their starting position, finishing position, points earned, and laps completed. races.csv was used only to get race names and filter the data down to just the 2021 season.

One key limitation is that driver names are not stored in the file — only ID numbers. I had to map those manually. The dataset also uses a special code called \N for missing values instead of leaving cells blank, which required extra cleaning.

03 — Data Cleaning & Preparation

Before any analysis I cleaned the data in five steps. Here is each step and the reason behind it.

  • 1
    Filtered to 2021 only — I started watching F1 from 2021, as it was lockdown times my friends group spoke a lot about it and that's when I started to follow the sport and the drivers like Max Verstappen, Lewis Hamilton and Lando Norris grabbed my attention as I like their teams too.

    Why: Analyzing 70 years of data at once would mix different eras of cars, rules, and teams — making any pattern meaningless. Isolating 2021 keeps the analysis focused on a single, consistent season where every driver raced under the same regulations.
  • 2
    Replaced \N with NaN — The Ergast dataset uses \N to indicate missing data. Python does not recognize this automatically, so I replaced it with NaN — the standard missing value format.

    Why: If \N is left as-is, Python treats it as a string — a piece of text, not a missing value. That means calculations silently break or return wrong results. Converting to NaN lets pandas handle gaps correctly and skip them when needed.
  • 3
    Converted columns to numbers — The points, grid, and position columns were being read as text. I converted them to numeric format so I could do calculations and comparisons.

    Why: A column full of "10", "6", "3" stored as text cannot be added, averaged, or sorted numerically. Python would treat them like words — "9" would sort before "10" alphabetically. Converting to numbers makes all math and charting behave as expected.
  • 4
    Added driver names — The results file only stores a numeric driver ID. I built a dictionary mapping each ID to the real driver name so charts would be readable.

    Why: A chart labeled "830" or "1" tells the viewer nothing. Replacing IDs with names like "Lewis Hamilton" or "Max Verstappen" makes every visualization immediately understandable without needing a separate lookup table.
  • 5
    Kept DNF rows intentionally — 52 rows have no finishing position because the driver retired. I kept these because retirements affect points and tell an important part of the story.

    Why: Dropping DNFs would silently inflate reliability stats and distort championship standings. A driver who retired 5 times looks very different from one who finished every race — removing those rows would erase that difference entirely and make the data misleading.

05 — Storytelling & Interpretation

Putting all five charts together, a clear story emerges. The 2021 season was fundamentally a two-driver battle — Chart 1 establishes this immediately, with Verstappen and Hamilton on a level nobody else came close to.

Chart 2 adds an important layer: qualifying was critical in 2021. A correlation of r = 0.75 between grid position and race finish tells us the championship was often shaped before the lights even went out. Teams that excelled in one-lap qualifying pace had a structural advantage going into every race.

Chart 4 shows the emotional version of this story. Hamilton led for most of the season, but Verstappen's momentum in the final rounds was unmistakable. The two lines converging toward Abu Dhabi makes the tension visible in a way that numbers alone cannot capture.

Chart 5 adds the final piece — survival. Every retirement changed the math. In a season decided by 8 points, a single extra DNF from either driver could have changed the champion entirely.

What would be misleading to conclude: We are measuring results, not driver talent in isolation. Verstappen and Hamilton dominated partly due to personal ability and partly due to having the two fastest cars. The data cannot separate those two things, which is an important limit on what we can claim.

06 — Limitations, Ethics & Reflection

What the data does not capture

The results file records outcomes but not causes. There is no information about weather, tire strategy, safety car timing, or car upgrades. Two identical-looking results in the data could have come from completely different situations on track.

A potential bias

My correlation analysis only included races where both grid and finish position were recorded — meaning DNF drivers were excluded. Chaotic races tend to produce both more position changes and more retirements. Excluding DNFs may make grid position look slightly more predictive than it really is.

The car versus driver problem

Points and positions reflect the combination of driver and car together. Drivers in better cars qualify higher and finish higher regardless of personal skill. Nothing in this dataset allows me to control for car performance.

What surprised me

I expected qualifying to be even more predictive. The r = 0.75 is strong, but the wide scatter shows that plenty of position changes happen across a full season. Races are genuinely unpredictable at an individual level, which is what makes F1 worth watching.

What I would explore next

If I had more time I would bring in lap_times.csv to analyze consistency of pace within individual races. That would get closer to separating driver ability from car performance — a driver managing tyre degradation well shows up in lap-by-lap data even when the final result does not reflect it.

Project 2 — DS2

Predicting Conflict Deaths Using Machine Learning

Regression modelling with Linear Regression · Decision Tree · Random Forest

01 — Problem Definition

Armed conflict data raises a sobering question: can we predict how deadly a year will be based purely on what types of wars are ongoing? This project attempts to answer that using machine learning on a global conflict deaths dataset.

The central research question:

  • ?Can we predict the total number of global conflict deaths in a year based on the types and number of ongoing wars?

This is a regression problem — the target is a continuous number (total deaths), not a category. Three models of increasing complexity were built and compared: Linear Regression, Decision Tree, and Random Forest. The goal was not just to build a model, but to understand whether the added complexity of each step was actually justified.

The dataset comes from Kaggle and is sourced from Our World in Data, which draws on the UCDP/PRIO Armed Conflict Dataset. Two files were merged.

deaths-in-armed-conflicts-by-type.csv — yearly global deaths broken down into five conflict types: one-sided, non-state, extrasystemic, intrastate, and interstate. number-of-wars-project-mars.csv — yearly count of ongoing civil wars and other wars, used as additional features.

Both files were merged on Entity (country/world) and Year. The inner join means any year missing from either file is lost — a limitation on an already small dataset. The final merged table had only around 23 rows, which is the most significant constraint on the entire project.

03 — Data Cleaning & Preparation

The data required four preparation steps before any modelling could begin.

  • 1
    Renamed columns — The original CSV column names were extremely long and unworkable in code. I shortened them to clean, consistent names like intrastate, civil_wars, and one_sided.

    Why: Long column names cause typos and make code unreadable. Renaming once at the top is far safer than repeating long strings throughout every cell. It also makes the code easier to explain and review.
  • 2
    Merged on Entity AND Year — Both files contain data for multiple countries. The merge was done on both columns together, not just Year alone.

    Why: Merging only on Year would match deaths from Rwanda in 1994 with war counts from a completely different country in 1994. Using both Entity and Year ensures every row represents the same place in the same year — a fundamental requirement for the data to mean anything.
  • 3
    Created total_deaths via feature engineering — The dataset has no pre-built total column. I constructed it by summing all five conflict-type death columns.

    Why: This is the target variable the model learns to predict. Without creating it manually, there is nothing to train toward. This step also introduces an important known limitation: because the target is literally the sum of the input features, correlation will always look artificially high — a fact I noted and reported honestly.
  • 4
    Dropped missing rows rather than filling them — Some rows had missing death counts. Instead of filling them with averages, I dropped those rows entirely.

    Why: Conflict death figures are sensitive real-world data. Inventing a value by filling with a column average would mean fabricating casualty estimates — which is both statistically wrong and ethically wrong for this kind of data. Dropping rows keeps the dataset honest, even though it further reduces an already small row count.

04 — Exploratory Data Analysis

Three charts were produced before any modelling, to understand the data and set expectations for what the models might find.

Chart 1 — Total Conflict Deaths Over Time Deaths peaked in the early 1990s — driven by conflicts like Rwanda and Bosnia — and have declined since. This wide range means the model needs to handle both very high and very low death counts across different years.
Chart 2 — Deaths by Conflict Type Intrastate deaths (civil wars) account for the largest share by far. This sets a clear expectation: the model should rank intrastate as its most important feature. If it does not, something is wrong.
Chart 3 — Correlation Heatmap Intrastate and one_sided show the strongest links to total_deaths. Note: because total_deaths is literally the sum of the input columns, correlations are artificially inflated — a known limitation stated openly.

06 — Storytelling & Interpretation

The three models together tell a clear progression. Linear Regression established the baseline — if even a straight line can explain most of the variation in deaths, the data has a strong underlying structure. Decision Tree added the ability to capture non-linear patterns, and Random Forest confirmed that the ensemble approach produces the most reliable results on a small dataset.

The feature importance chart is where the project connects back to the real world. Intrastate deaths — civil wars — being the most predictive feature is not just a machine learning result. It reflects post-Cold War history: the 1990s were defined by internal conflicts, not wars between nations.

What would be misleading to conclude: All three models score high partly because total_deaths is mathematically built from the input features — it is literally their sum. That means a model that simply adds them up will always look accurate. This is called target leakage, and it means these R² scores overstate how well the models would generalise to truly new data.

07 — Limitations, Ethics & Reflection

Dataset size

Only ~23 rows remained after merging. This is far too small for reliable machine learning. A 5-row test set means a single unusual data point can swing all metrics dramatically. All results should be treated as exploratory rather than conclusive.

Target leakage

The target variable (total_deaths) is the sum of the five conflict-type death columns used as features. This means any model that simply adds those columns will score near-perfectly. High R² scores in this project are partly a structural artefact, not purely a signal of predictive power.

Ethics of conflict data

This dataset represents real deaths in real conflicts. Treating casualty figures as rows in a training set requires care. I chose to drop missing values rather than impute them precisely because inventing conflict death estimates would be ethically wrong, regardless of how statistically convenient it might be.

What surprised me

Hyperparameter tuning showed that limiting tree depth genuinely matters on small datasets. An unconstrained Decision Tree overfits immediately — the cross-validation results made this clear in a way that a single train/test split would not have revealed.

What I would explore next

A larger dataset spanning more countries individually (rather than global totals) would make this a much more meaningful project. I would also explore predicting conflict type rather than just death count — a classification problem rather than regression.

Why Learning Data Science in Social and Interdisciplinary Contexts Matters

Learning data science in social and interdisciplinary contexts matters because data does not exist in a vacuum. Behind every dataset involving people are real lives, experiences, and consequences. Modeling social topics allows us to better understand patterns in behavior, inequality, communication, and decision-making, but it also forces us to confront the limitations of purely technical approaches. Numbers can describe trends, but interpretation requires social awareness, ethical reasoning, and contextual understanding.

Modeling people and social systems can help answer important questions, such as how resources are distributed, how information spreads, or how policies affect different groups. However, these models can also oversimplify human behavior if we are not careful. People are not just data points, and social systems are influenced by culture, history, and power structures that may not be fully captured in a dataset. This makes interdisciplinary thinking essential when working with social data.

With the use of data about people comes responsibility. One responsibility is protecting privacy and ensuring that individuals are not harmed or exposed through data misuse. Another is avoiding biased conclusions that reinforce stereotypes or systemic inequalities. Data scientists must think critically about where data comes from, who is represented or excluded, and how results might be used in practice. Transparency, fairness, and accountability are key responsibilities when working with social data.

Through this course, I hope to better understand how data science tools can be applied thoughtfully to social questions. I want to learn how to analyze data while considering ethical implications and real-world impact. I am especially interested in gaining skills that allow me to communicate findings clearly and responsibly, making data-driven insights accessible and meaningful beyond purely technical audiences.

Another reason learning data science in social and interdisciplinary contexts matters is because data-driven tools often influence real decisions that affect people's lives. Models are used to shape policies, recommend actions, and predict outcomes, which means mistakes or oversights can have real consequences. This has made me realize that building an accurate model is not always enough. It's just as important to think about the assumptions behind the data and what might be missing from it. A model might perform well technically but still lead to unfair results if social context is ignored. Through this course, I hope to become more comfortable questioning results, recognizing limitations, and thinking carefully about how data-driven conclusions impact different groups of people.
